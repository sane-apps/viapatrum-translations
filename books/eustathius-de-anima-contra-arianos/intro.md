Eustathius of Antioch (fl. c. 320–337) wrote this treatise for no named addressee against Arian claims that Christ assumed a body without a human soul.

Eustathius argues from Christ's birth, growth, suffering, death and resurrection that he possessed a real human body with a soul, pointing to his infancy and to the wounds, the pierced side, the nail prints, the blood and the signs of death as evidence of bodily reality and to Christ's soul leading the thief's soul into paradise while his body lay in the tomb as proof of the soul's distinct reality. He argues against the Arians, who deny Christ a soul in order to attach the changes and passions to the divine Spirit and to persuade people that what is changeable could not be born from the unchangeable nature. The reader will also meet Stephen, who beheld the divine glory, Herod, whose plot forced the infant Christ's flight to Egypt, Moses and Samuel as intercessors, the thief crucified with Christ, and Jeremiah, who is quoted introducing God's words with 'And the Lord said to me, saying'.

As rendered here, soul means the human rational soul, Word means the divine Word united to Christ's human nature, tabernacle means Christ's body as the dwelling of the Word, body means his physical body, passion means his suffering and death, resurrection means his bodily resurrection, paradise means the place promised to the thief, changeable means what can change and unchangeable nature means the divine nature, and Spirit means the divine Spirit. The author cites the Septuagint with Greek forms such as Mōysēs, Samouēl and Hieremias, Psalm numbering follows the Septuagint, and citations are given in modern form with LXX book names where they differ.
