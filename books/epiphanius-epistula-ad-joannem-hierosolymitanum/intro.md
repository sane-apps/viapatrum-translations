Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 310/320–403) was bishop of Salamis, also called Constantia, in Cyprus. He wrote this letter in Greek. It is one of the few pieces of his correspondence that survive through Jerome, who rendered it into Latin at his request and kept it in his own collection.

He sent it to John, bishop of Jerusalem, to explain and defend his act of tearing a curtain painted with a human image in a church at Anautha and to send a replacement. Behind it lay John's anger because Epiphanius had ordained a priest, Jerome's brother Paulinian, inside John's jurisdiction, while the wider Origenist dispute was at its height and Epiphanius had openly charged John with being an Origenist. The letter reads as a half apology that only made matters worse.

Epiphanius urges John not to be carried away by pride of office, where office means clerical office, but to heed Scripture, prays that God will crush Satan under the feet of Christians and keep the bond of love, peace, right faith and truth unbroken, then tells how on his way to meet John at Bethel he tore the idol-like curtain as an abomination and said it should wrap a poor dead person. The reader also hears the grumblers, quoted in direct speech to say he should have supplied a replacement from his own store before he tore it. Here curtain is the cloth hung in a church, human image means a painted person, honor is the form used to address John, church means building and people, and the warning against grumbling is quoted from 1 Corinthians 10:10 without naming Paul, with a hint of 10:9.
