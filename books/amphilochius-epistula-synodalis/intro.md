Amphilochius of Iconium (c. 340–c. 395) was bishop of Iconium and writes here on behalf of a synod over which he presided. He answers a common letter from a distant church or group of churches that asked the synod for a ruling on the faith, especially concerning the Holy Spirit. Because Basil was kept from attending by illness, Amphilochius replies in the synod's name and sends Basil's own treatise on the subject with his letter.

The letter affirms the Nicene faith as truly catholic and apostolic and explains that the fathers said little about the Spirit because that question was still silenced at the time. It grounds the Trinity in Christ's baptismal command naming Father, Son and Holy Spirit, and argues the Spirit cannot stand between creator and creation, so to cut him off from the Godhead numbers him with creatures and makes baptism incoherent. It urges the recipients to remain united in peace, to glorify the Spirit with Father and Son, and warns that those who blaspheme the Spirit will face condemnation with the Arians. The reader meets Basil, whose treatise is enclosed, Arius, whose heresy the Nicene fathers sought to cut off, and Sabellius, whose error the three persons shut out, while the Arians, the Anomoeans and the opponents of the Spirit are argued against; Christ's command is quoted in direct discourse and a proverbial saying about the wise man is also quoted.

To follow the argument, read person for each of Father, Son and Holy Spirit, nature for the one divine nature they share, and Godhead for the divine standing they fully share. Creature means a made thing, creation means all made things, and creator means the maker, while Anomoeans say the Son is unlike the Father and opponents of the Spirit deny the Spirit a place in the Godhead. The baptismal command is cited from Matthew by its modern name, with no old book names or special numbering, and blasphemy against the Spirit is linked with the unforgivable sin spoken of in the Gospels.
