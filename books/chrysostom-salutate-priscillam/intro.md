John Chrysostom (c. 349–407) wrote these two homilies for his congregation. He preached them on the apostolic reading Romans 16:3, where Paul says, 'Greet Priscilla and Aquila.' He wanted to show that even a bare greeting in Scripture is not superfluous and to correct his hearers' neglect of Scripture, their contempt for manual labor and poverty, and, in the second homily, their slander of clergy.

Chrysostom argues that no part of Scripture, even a simple greeting, is superfluous. From Paul's greeting to Priscilla and Aquila he draws lessons about true nobility, the dignity of marriage and manual labor, and the virtue of hospitality. He then defends the apostles against the charge of disobeying Christ's command to possess nothing, explaining that the command was temporary and for their training. Finally, he turns to the duty of honoring priests and warns against slandering spiritual fathers, using biblical examples to show the danger of judging others. The reader will meet Paul, Priscilla, Aquila, Apollos, Peter, Timothy, John the Baptist, Elijah, Moses, Miriam, David, Saul, Ahab, Herod, Epaphras, Abraham, Carpus, Persis, Mary, and Tryphaena. Chrysostom quotes Paul, Christ, the disciples, and God directly, and he stages an objector who voices common objections.

A reader should know that Chrysostom quotes the Septuagint and the Greek New Testament. Psalms are cited by their LXX numbering, which is usually one behind the Hebrew numbering. The books of Kingdoms (1–4 Kingdoms) correspond to 1–2 Samuel and 1–2 Kings. Key terms rendered in this translation include greeting for a personal salutation in Paul's letters, tentmakers for manual laborers who worked with leather, coworkers for fellow laborers in the gospel, hospitality for welcoming holy people and strangers, idleness for the root of many evils, work for manual occupation, poverty for lack of possessions presented as freedom, nobility for virtue-based excellence rather than ancestry, priests for clergy and spiritual fathers, slander for speaking evil especially of clergy, churches for local Christian communities, discipline for the Christian way of life and ascetic practice, boldness for freedom of speech in rebuking the powerful, virtue for excellence of soul, and disposition for deliberate choice.
