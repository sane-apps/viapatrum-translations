Origen of Alexandria (c. 185–c. 254) wrote this treatise to address a disputed question about the parable of Lazarus and the rich man, specifically whether its details imply bodily resurrection or describe the soul's state before the resurrection. The surviving fragments include material preserved by Methodius, a later opponent of Origen, and at least one fragment may reflect Methodius's framing of Origen's position.

Origen reports two existing readings of the parable: simpler readers take it as bodily reward and punishment, while more precise readers deny this because after the resurrection no one remains in this life, as shown by the rich man's reference to his five living brothers. Origen tentatively suggests that the soul at its departure may take on a form like the coarse earthly body, which would explain how departed figures such as Samuel can appear visibly. He argues that the rich man being punished in Hades and Lazarus resting in Abraham's bosom, both before the Savior's coming and before the resurrection, teach that even now the soul uses a body after its departure. A fragment preserved by Methodius presents Origen as declaring that since those who attain the resurrection will be like angels, and angels exist without flesh in the height of blessedness, those made equal to angels must likewise be without flesh; this may be Methodius's hostile characterization or paraphrase of Origen's view. The reader will meet Lazarus, the rich man, Abraham, Samuel, and the Sadducees, who test Jesus about the resurrection, as well as Methodius who preserves and possibly distorts Origen's words.

Key terms in this translation include: "resurrection" for the future rising of the dead at the end of the age; "soul" for the incorporeal substance that departs the body at death; "body" for both the coarse earthly body and the body-like form the soul may take after death; "Hades" for the place of punishment before the resurrection; "Abraham's bosom" for the place of rest before the resurrection; "angels" who exist without flesh and to whom the resurrected will be made equal; "departure" for the soul's separation from the body at death; "incorporeal" for the soul's substance without a body; "flesh" for the coarse earthly substance that angels lack and the resurrected will not have; and "end of the age" for the future consummation before which the intermediate state exists. Scripture references use LXX book names: the parable is from Luke 16:19-31; Samuel's appearance to Saul is from 1 Kingdoms 28 (1 Samuel 28 in modern Bibles); and Jesus' saying about the resurrection and the Sadducees is from Matthew 22:30, Mark 12:25, and Luke 20:35-36. The Sadducees were a Jewish group who denied the resurrection and tested Jesus on this point.
