Philostorgius (c. 368–c. 439) was an Anomoean church historian of the Eunomian branch, a nonconformist faction opposed to the Christianity adopted as the official religion of the empire. He wrote with no named addressee, continuing and correcting Eusebius from an Arian perspective.

He wrote his history as a revisionist account of the church and the empire, sometimes contradicting and often supplementing other histories. The original is lost and reaches readers through the epitome by Photius, who condemns him throughout as heretical and impious. In it he assesses earlier Jewish and Christian writings, praising the first book of Maccabees, rejecting the third and saying Eusebius erred about piety though praiseworthy in other respects, traces the quarrel between Alexander the bishop of Alexandria and the presbyter Arius to the presbyter Alexander nicknamed Baucalis in second rank and says the proclamation of same substance was contrived there, and records the death of Constantius and the succession and conversion of Constantine after victory over Daxentius. A reader will meet Judas Maccabeus, Antiochus Epiphanes and Antiochus Eupator, Jason of Cyrene, Josephus, Eleazar, the pagan historians Phlegon and Dion, Eusebius, Arius and the two men named Alexander, Constantius, Constantine, Diocletian and Daxentius, with earlier accounts summarized and set aside where he disagrees.

Same substance here means the doctrine that the Son is of the same substance as the Father, bishopric means the office at Alexandria whose votes Arius yielded to Alexander, piety means true piety or right worship, and second rank marks Baucalis as subordinate to the bishop. The books are cited as 1 Maccabees, 2 Maccabees, 3 Maccabees and 4 Maccabees with Daniel, while the first of Kingdoms appears as 1 Samuel and Jesus son of Nave as Joshua. Phlegon and Dion are pagan historians who only briefly mentioned Jewish events, Eleazar belongs among the Maccabean martyrs, Diocletian is the emperor whose plot Constantine escaped, Constantius ruled as emperor in the upper Galatias where the Alps are, Eusebius is identified as associated with Pamphilus, and Daxentius keeps the manuscript spelling for the rival defeated by Constantine.
