This homily is attributed to Basil of Caesarea (c. 330–379). He is the first-person preacher who delivers it, although the text itself does not name him.

He preaches to a gathered congregation at Lakizoi, a large assembly, urging them to resist distraction and to profit from the morning scriptural readings by applying each text as a remedy for particular sins. He expounds the proverb as a call to impartiality toward rich and poor, friend and stranger, and then treats anger, envy and greed as destructive passions for which each passage supplies a cure. The reader will meet the preacher himself, the devil who envied the honor of humanity and still attacks God through God's image in human beings, and the man who fell among robbers, read as the soul. The words quoted throughout are those of Scripture, chiefly Proverbs.

Impartiality here means even-handed treatment of persons and partiality means favoritism. Envy and greed name begrudging another's good and taking more than one's due, while wound and stripping name sins that wound the soul and its loss of grace, and restoration and new birth name return to blessedness and baptismal washing. Proverbs is quoted in its Septuagint form, the man who fell among robbers alludes to Luke 10:30 and is read as the soul going down from Jerusalem to Jericho, and Psalms follow Septuagint numbering, cited here by standard English Bible book names.
