Jerome (c. 340/345–420) is the translator writing in Latin and the author of these prefaces.

He writes for Christian readers generally, with the Daniel preface addressed to Paula, a Roman Christian noblewoman and his patron and correspondent, and to her daughter Eustochium, also his patron and correspondent, to defend and introduce his new Latin translations from the Hebrew Scriptures and to explain the Hebrew canon, his method, and why he excludes or marks additions found in Greek versions. He argues that the canon holds twenty two Hebrew books matching the twenty two letters of the Hebrew alphabet and that books beyond them are apocryphal books, and he defends translating from the original Hebrew rather than the Septuagint without changing it, separates canonical books from edifying books such as Wisdom and Sirach, notes that the churches use Theodotion's version of Daniel rather than the Septuagint without knowing why, and notes that Susanna, the hymn of the three youths, and Bel and the Dragon are not in the Hebrew Daniel and have been given a mark and placed at the end. A reader will meet Porphyry, a pagan philosopher who wrote against Daniel and was answered by the Christian writers Methodius, Eusebius and Apollinaris, and Africanus and Origen in their argument about Susanna, and will hear a Hebrew teacher reported indirectly and Paul the Apostle quoted directly on 2 Corinthians 12.

To follow it, a reader should know that canon means the authoritative list of scriptural books, apocryphal books means books beyond the Hebrew canon and not used for establishing doctrine, the original Hebrew means the Hebrew text against Greek versions, the Septuagint means the Greek translation of the Old Testament, and Theodotion's version means the Greek Daniel used by the churches. The Hebrew Scriptures are divided into the Law, the Prophets and the Writings, with parts of Daniel and Ezra in Aramaic, and older titles appear here as Chronicles, Kings, Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy, Joshua son of Nun, Judges, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Song of Songs, Lamentations, the Twelve Prophets and Sirach. Psalms follow Hebrew numbering, so Psalm 38 in Hebrew is Psalm 39 in Septuagint numbering.
