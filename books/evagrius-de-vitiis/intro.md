Evagrius Ponticus (c. 345–399) wrote this treatise for a fellow ascetic or disciple addressed as your diligence, to complete his previous instruction on the virtues with a concise catalogue of the vices opposed to them. He presents the work as a summary of sound teaching received from the Fathers and not as his own achievement.

Evagrius begins by acknowledging that his insight comes from grace and not from his own works and by asking pardon for his boldness in teaching, then pairs gluttony with self-control, sexual immorality with chastity, love of money with poverty, grief with joy, anger with patience, listlessness with endurance, vainglory with freedom from vainglory, and pride with humility. The reader will meet Evagrius himself, named in the manuscripts as Nilus of Ancyra, and the Fathers, the earlier monastic teachers from whom he says he received this teaching. The only words quoted are those of divine grace, introduced as spoken by grace, and no opposing voice is quoted or argued against.

To follow the text it helps to know that the Fathers are the elder monks whose teaching Evagrius passes on, and that most vices and virtues are described through chains of vivid epithets, except pride, which has no chain of its own. This translation uses gluttony, self-control, sexual immorality, chastity, love of money, poverty, grief, joy, anger, patience, listlessness, endurance, vainglory, freedom from vainglory, envy, pride and humility. The work names no books of the Bible and offers only unattributed quotations and allusions, and it closes with vainglory linked to pride and envy and a prayer that Christ may prosper every work of God's will.
