Gregory Thaumaturgus (c. 213–c. 270) was bishop of Neocaesarea in Pontus, also known as Gregory of Neocaesarea and called the wonder-worker. Born to a pagan family and originally named Theodore, he studied with Origen at Caesarea in Palestine. He wrote this treatise to his friend Tatian, who was already instructed in divine Scriptures and traditions.

Tatian asked Gregory for a discourse and disputation on the soul that would use effective arguments and proofs and no scriptural testimonies. Tatian did not seek it for his own assurance, for he was already taught by Holy Scriptures and traditions, but to convince those who think otherwise and refuse belief to the divine Scriptures, including philosophers and heretics with whom Christians daily came into conflict, who use skill in disputation to circumvent and deceive people unaccustomed to such debate. Gregory complied, trusting in Tatian's goodwill, and promised to follow the customary order of inquiry with common notions alone, asking first by what criterion the soul can be grasped according to nature, then whether it exists, whether it is substance or accident, body or incorporeal, simple or composite, mortal or immortal, and whether it is rational or not. Apart from Tatian as addressee and Gregory as author, no other speakers appear and no opposing words are quoted for refutation.

To follow the inquiry, a reader needs the renderings used here. Soul is the subject throughout, proofs are arguments without scriptural testimonies, and common notions are the general conceptions by which assent is given even to things handled by the hands. Substance is posed against accident, where accident means what depends on another to exist and not a mishap, and the soul is then asked if it is body or incorporeal, simple or composite, mortal or immortal, and rational or not, starting from the criterion by which it can be grasped according to nature. Gregory uses no scriptural testimonies here, so no names of Bible books need to be learned.
