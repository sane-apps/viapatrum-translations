Basil of Caesarea (c. 330–379) wrote this homily for unbaptized hearers, especially catechumens and those who have long delayed baptism. He delivered it as a sermon urging immediate reception of the sacrament, arguing that every moment of human life is the right time but that Easter is especially fitting because it commemorates the resurrection and baptism is power for resurrection.

The work opens with Solomon's wisdom on times and seasons, then contrasts John the Baptist's baptism of repentance with Christ's baptism of adoption. Basil answers the common excuse of postponing baptism until old age or after enjoying sin, exposing it as the devil's trick and a dangerous gamble with death. He quotes the Lord's words, invokes Elijah's water-drenched sacrifice and the eunuch's immediate baptism, and warns through the imagined lament of the dying unbaptized that delay risks eternal loss.

Key terms rendered in this translation include rebirth (spiritual regeneration), seal (the mark identifying one as Christ's), adoption (becoming God's child), enlightenment (another name for baptism), repentance (turning from sin), grace (God's gift of forgiveness and new life), freedom (release from slavery to sin and the devil), enrollment (registration among the Church and citizens of heaven), garment of light (the bright garment of baptism symbolizing new pure life), kingdom of heaven (the promised inheritance), sinful nature (the mindset hostile to God), pleasure (bodily pleasure that enslaves), and sin (understood as a debt and slavery). Scripture quotations follow the Septuagint with book names and Psalm numbering converted to standard English forms.
