Gregory Thaumaturgus (c. 213–c. 270) wrote Twelve Chapters on the Faith as a treatise that defends orthodox Christology against positions that either deny the createdness of Christ's body or confuse his flesh with his divinity.

He argues that Christ's body is created, not uncreated, because what is uncreated cannot suffer, be wounded or be touched, yet the risen Christ showed the nails and the wound made by the lance to the disciples and presented his body able to be touched when entering through closed doors. He also argues that Christ's flesh is not consubstantial with the divinity, since that means identity of nature and eternal existence while the flesh exists in time, and he affirms that Christ is truly God and truly man. The reader meets Christ as the incarnate Word, and no other writer is quoted by name; the positions opposed are presented only as errors to be rejected and the work cites Philippians 2:6-7.

To follow it a reader needs only a few renderings used here. Uncreated means having no beginning and not subject to suffering, and incapable of suffering names that property, while consubstantial means of the same nature and eternal existence. Flesh means what is created in Christ and exists in time, divinity means what is eternal in Christ, form of a servant and emptied himself come from Philippians 2:6-7, and let him be anathema is the formula of condemnation for holding the stated error. Bible references follow modern form such as Philippians 2:6-7, and the resurrection details about nails, the lance wound, touch and closed doors are combined without naming a Gospel account.
