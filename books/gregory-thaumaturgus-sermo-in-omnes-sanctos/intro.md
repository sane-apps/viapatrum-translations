Gregory Thaumaturgus (c. 213–c. 270) is named as the author of this homily.

He speaks to a gathered congregation celebrating a festival of the holy martyrs. He wishes to remain silent because his speech is unpolished, but the visible splendor of the festal assembly in honor of the holy martyrs and the zeal of the congregation move him to speak.

The martyrs draw old men, women with young men, and infants to the feast, and leap against death, laugh at swords, plunder the rage of the tyrant, seize death as the agent of immortality, and make victory their own in their fall. They also leap through their bodies toward heaven, scatter their limbs to bind their souls, and break the bars of life to open the keys of heaven. A reader will meet Christ as victor over Hades and death, the holy martyrs, and the devil stripped of his ancient armor and cast out from his own dominion. An unidentified voice is quoted as a taunt to death and Hades, "Where, O death, is your sting? Where, O Hades, your victory?", from Hosea and 1 Corinthians. Here festival means the festal assembly, Hades means the realm of the dead, tyrant means the devil or death as a ruling hold, sting means its wound, armor means the devil's old battle gear now stripped away, and dominion means his own ruling place.
