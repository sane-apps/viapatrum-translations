Tertullian (c. 155–c. 220) was a Christian teacher in Carthage and one of the earliest Christian authors to write in Latin. He wrote this treatise to the virgins of the church and to the wider Christian community, probably around the year 204. His purpose was to defend the practice of veiling virgins in church against those who said that local custom allowed them to remain unveiled.

The occasion was a dispute in Carthage. Some Christians argued that because the apostle Paul mentioned only women and not virgins when he taught about veiling, virgins were not required to cover their heads. Tertullian answers that truth, not custom, must govern church discipline, and that the Holy Spirit continues to lead the church into fuller truth. He insists that the word woman in Scripture includes virgins, and that Paul's reasons for veiling women apply equally to them.

A reader will meet Eve, called woman while still a virgin, and Mary, whom Paul and the angel Gabriel also called woman. Tertullian quotes Paul, Christ, Genesis, and Ecclesiastes, and he argues against opponents whose words he quotes directly. He also recalls Rebecca, who veiled herself when she saw Isaac from afar. To follow the argument, know that veil means the head covering worn in church, woman is the general term for the female sex including virgins, and custom means established practice, which Tertullian contrasts with truth. Scripture is cited from the Old Latin version, which follows the Greek Septuagint, so psalm numbering sometimes differs from modern English Bibles.
