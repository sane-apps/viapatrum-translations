Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 310/320–403) was born near Eleutheropolis in Palestine and served for nearly forty years as bishop of Constantia, which is Salamis. While bishop he wrote this treatise for a general Christian readership, with one closing fragment addressed to Diodorus. He wrote at the request of Diodorus, bishop of Tyre, to explain the twelve stones set in the priest's breastplate.

He takes up the twelve stones on the High Priest's breastplate described in Exodus, giving for each its color, place of origin and reputed power, including the story of the hyacinth brought from a deep Scythian ravine by eagles carrying meat watched by condemned laborers. In a closing fragment he turns to the Samaritans, tracing Mount Somoron to Shemir, a son of the Canaanites, and describing the Samaritans as guards who received the Pentateuch through Ezra and are set apart from the descendants of Abraham. The reader also meets Diodorus, the addressee of that fragment, and hears the words of Scripture, quoted directly, about the Samaritans keeping God's law while worshipping idols, which Epiphanius then explains by the idols foreign priests hid on Mount Garizim toward which the Samaritans pray.

Here breastplate means the priest's breastplate or oracle holding the twelve stones, power means a stone's healing or protective property used by doctors, and giving of the law means the lawgiving to Moses on Sinai said to have appeared on sapphire. Samaritans means guards, who kept only the Pentateuch, the five books of Moses, while idols means the figures foreign priests hid on Mount Garizim toward which the Samaritans pray. The author uses Septuagint book names, so the passage on the Samaritans keeping God's law and worshipping idols is cited in modern form as 2 Kings 17:32-33, and the heading naming Diodorus marks the shift to the closing fragment on Samaritan origins.
